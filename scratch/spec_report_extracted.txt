2. The Problem: Current Process & Inefficiencies 
Jeffway Construction's current process for electrical estimating and commercial tracking relies heavily on 
manual data extraction and spreadsheet management. This creates a series of tangible business 
challenges that directly impact commercial capacity and margin protection. 
The core of the problem begins at the tender stage. When a tender pack estimators must manually 
review extensive architectural drawing packs (often 40+ pages per house type), counting every single 
socket, light fitting, and cable run by hand. This data is then manually transcribed into complex, multi-tab 
spreadsheets separating 1st Fix, 2nd Fix, and Communals. The process of obtaining supplier quotes 
involves sending out lists to up to six different suppliers (including Templegate, Medlock, and KEW), 
waiting for returns in varying formats, and manually comparing line items. Once a project is won, the 
burden shifts to the commercial team, who must manually process handwritten PDF timesheets from

This manual dependency creates a cascade of further inefficiencies: 
●​ Estimating Bottlenecks & Capacity Constraints: The time-intensive nature of manual drawing 
takeoffs means estimators spend hours counting symbols on architectural elevations and floor plans 
rather than strategising on pricing or negotiating better rates. This limits the volume of tenders the

●​ Margin Erosion through Pricing Errors: Manually comparing supplier quotes across six different

when suppliers fail to apply agreed discounts on their monthly invoices, the manual checking process 
often misses these discrepancies, leading to direct financial loss. 
●​ Labour Tracking Inaccuracies: Processing handwritten PDF timesheets manually and 
cross-referencing them against the complex labour tab (which splits pricing across 10 categories like 
Ducting, 1st Fix, Finals, and EV Chargers) is highly prone to error. Operatives using inconsistent

overpayments and skewed project profitability data. 
●​ Lack of Performance Visibility: Because the data is locked in disparate spreadsheets, PDFs, and 
EQUE2, it is incredibly difficult to identify trends, such as which operatives consistently take longer on 
specific house types, or which plots are consistently underpriced at the tender stage. 
 
3. The Solution: A Bespoke AI Commercial Assistant 
We will build a bespoke AI-powered digital assistant that operates directly across your existing estimating

The automation will utilise advanced AI vision capabilities (specifically GPT-4o Vision) to read full

tireless commercial guardian, protecting your margins and freeing your human team to focus on strategic 
project management and negotiation.

5. Implementation Notes 
Our investigation confirms that the core workflow relies on complex architectural PDF packs, handwritten 
PDF timesheets, and highly structured, multi-tab Excel spreadsheets. The automation will therefore be 
built using a two-step approach for document reading: a dedicated OCR engine (such as Google 
Document AI or Azure Document Intelligence) to extract the text first, then passing it to GPT-4o Vision for 
normalisation, spatial understanding, and logic. This two-step approach gives significantly better accuracy 
than vision alone, especially on handwritten timesheets or dense architectural elevations. AI vision 
models are very capable at reading architectural drawings and counting electrical components, and this is 
well-established technology (dedicated tools like Drawer AI already do this commercially with up to 70% 
time savings), so we are confident in the approach. 
We will establish a dedicated SharePoint or OneDrive folder structure to act as the trigger point for new 
tender uploads. The AI will be trained to recognise your specific spreadsheet formats (including the 
173-row Communals tab and the 10-category Labour Prices sheet) to ensure seamless data population. 
Crucially, tasks requiring commercial negotiation or final sign-off (such as challenging a supplier on price 
or approving a final tender letter) are explicitly preserved as human-in-the-loop checkpoints.

6. Full, Unabridged Automation Breakdown 
This section details every step the AI agent will perform. This is the definitive scope of work. 
Module 1: AI-Powered Electrical Plan Takeoff 
●​ Trigger: An estimator uploads a new tender pack (architectural PDFs and specifications) into a 
designated 'New Tenders' SharePoint folder, and the project is logged in the new build pipeline. 
●​ Step 1.1: Document Parsing & Page Identification: The agent scans the uploaded architectural packs 
(often 40+ pages per house type, including substructure plans, setting out plans, joists plans, roof 
plans, elevations, sections, and schedules). It intelligently identifies and extracts only the relevant 
floor plans (e.g., 'Ground Floor Setting Out', 'First Floor Setting Out') while ignoring elevations, roof 
truss details, staircase details, and substructure plans that do not contain electrical data. It also reads 
the cover sheet to identify the house type name, the applicable plots, the specification level (e.g., 
Lovell Gold, Lovell Silver, Shared Ownership, Rented), and whether the build is Masonry or Timber 
Frame. 
●​ Step 1.2: Symbol Key Detection: If the tender pack includes a symbol legend or key, the agent ingests 
it and maps each symbol to a component category. If no key is provided, the agent defaults to the 
standard BS 1192 / IEC electrical symbol library. The estimator can supply a custom key at any time, 
which the agent will store for future use with that developer or architect. 
●​ Step 1.3: AI Vision Extraction (Houses): For standard housing plots, the agent processes the 
identified floor plans for each house type (e.g., Giganti, Crocus, Conte, Lunaria, Violet). Using GPT-4o 
Vision, it scans the plan room-by-room, counting every electrical component: single sockets, double 
sockets, USB sockets, fused spur units, light switches (1-gang, 2-gang, 3-gang, dimmer), ceiling 
pendants, recessed downlights, extractor fans, cooker switches, cooker control units, double-pole 
switches, consumer units, MCBs, RCBOs, smoke detectors, heat detectors, CO detectors, shaver 
sockets, TV plates, data plates, oven connections, hob connections, ASHP connections, EV charger 
points, and any PV connections. Each item is logged against the room it appears in. 
●​ Step 1.4: AI Vision Extraction (Flats): For flat developments, the agent processes each flat type 
drawing individually (e.g., Apartment M1A GF, Apartment M1B GF, Apartment M1C FF), performing 
the same room-by-room component count as Step 1.3. Each flat type is treated as a distinct unit with 
its own takeoff. The agent also identifies the floor level (Ground Floor, First Floor, Second Floor) for 
each flat type. 
●​ Step 1.5: Communal Area Separation: For blocks of flats, the agent identifies and separates 
communal areas (entrance lobbies, corridors, stairwells, landings, bin stores, cycle stores, plant 
rooms, retail units) from individual apartments. Communal components are logged into their own 
dedicated section, broken down by floor and area. This includes communal lighting (emergency and 
standard), small power (landlord sockets), distribution boards, submains cabling, containment (cable 
tray, basket, trunking), TV aerial distribution, door entry and intercom systems, fire alarm devices, 
and AOV (Automatic Opening Vent) systems.

●​ Step 1.6: Large Block Sectioning: For large-scale developments, the agent sections the communal 
takeoff into the standard Jeffway categories: Design, Prelims, Distribution, BNO Works, Containment, 
Small Power, and Lighting. Each section maps directly to the corresponding area in the Communals 
tab of the estimating spreadsheet. Within each section, the agent separates Materials (with 
quantities and costs) from Labour (with estimated hours and labour costs). 
●​ Step 1.7: Spreadsheet Population (House Type Tabs): The agent opens the Jeffway estimating 
template and populates the individual house type tabs (e.g., 'Giganti', 'Crocus', 'Conte', 'Lunaria', 
'Violet'). For each tab, it fills in the Qty, Manufacturer (defaulting to Deta where applicable), Part 
Number, Description, Unit Price (from historical data or left blank for supplier pricing), and Total 
columns. The structure follows the existing format: consumer units and MCBs first, then sockets, 
switches, light fittings, accessories, and specialist items. 
●​ Step 1.8: Spreadsheet Population (First Fix): The agent populates the master '1st Fix' tab with all 
cable quantities (e.g., 6242Y 2.5mm, 6242Y 1.5mm, SWA), back boxes (1-gang, 2-gang, circular), 
fixings, and sundries. These are categorised across the columns by house type, matching the existing 
47-column layout. 
●​ Step 1.9: Spreadsheet Population (Communals): For flat developments, the agent populates the 
'Communals' tab, filling in quantities across all subsections: Submains, Power, Low Voltage & Aerial, 
Lighting, AOV, Door Entry, Bin Stores, Cycle Stores, and Retail Units. It calculates both material costs 
and labour hours for each section. 
●​ Step 1.10: Accommodation Schedule Cross-Reference: The agent reads the Accommodation 
Schedule provided in the tender pack (listing every plot number, house type, beds, tenure, 
specification, and garage type) and uses this to calculate site-wide totals. It multiplies the per-type 
takeoff quantities by the number of plots of each type to produce the aggregated figures for the 
master 'Tender' summary tab. 
●​ Step 1.11: Special Requirements Check: The agent checks the specification and drawings for special 
requirements including EV charging (identifying which plots require it and whether it is on-dwelling 
or separate), solar PV connections, attached vs. detached garages (with different electrical 
requirements), site compound electrical requirements, and any sales area or show home 
specifications. 
●​ Step 1.12: Discrepancy Flagging: The agent cross-references the written specification against the 
drawings. If a discrepancy is found (e.g., spec mandates bathroom downlights but drawings show 
pendants, or spec requires a specific manufacturer but drawings show a generic symbol), it generates 
an alert note on the summary tab for the estimator to query with the developer before finalising. 
●​ Step 1.13: Estimator Review Checkpoint: The agent pauses the workflow and sends a notification to 
the estimator that the takeoff is complete and ready for review. The estimator reviews the quantities 
in the spreadsheet and provides approval to proceed to pricing. This is the critical safety net before 
anything feeds into the commercial stages. 
Module 2: Labour Pricing & Estimation 
●​ Trigger: The estimator reviews and approves the completed takeoff from Module 1. 
●​ Step 2.1: Historical Data Retrieval: The agent accesses the historical pricing database, pulling labour 
costs from previous tenders of comparable size and specification (e.g., previous Lovell, Crest 
Nicholson, or Taylor Wimpey projects). It retrieves the standard labour times from the Luckins 
reference data for each component type.

●​ Step 2.2: Labour Calculation (Houses): For each house type, the agent calculates the base labour 
cost broken down by the 10 standard Jeffway categories: Ducting, 1st Fix, 2nd Fix, Finals & Heating, 
Fit Appliances, Weather Compensator, 20mm Condensate Pipe, Test, EV Charger, and Garages. The 
calculation is based on the total component count for that house type multiplied by the standard 
labour time, then multiplied by the agreed rate. 
●​ Step 2.3: Labour Calculation (Communal): For communal areas in flat developments, the agent 
calculates labour costs by section (Containment, Lighting, Small Power, Distribution, AOV, Door Entry, 
etc.), using the agreed hourly rate (e.g., £20/hour as per the existing Communals tab) multiplied by 
the estimated hours per section. It produces both a per-section cost and a total communal labour 
package figure. 
●​ Step 2.4: Historical Comparison: The agent compares the calculated labour price against actual costs 
from previous projects of similar size and complexity. If the calculated figure deviates significantly 
from historical actuals (e.g., the tender says £400 per 1st Fix for a Crocus but previous actuals 
averaged £450), the agent flags this for the estimator's attention with a clear recommendation. 
●​ Step 2.5: Markup Application: The agent applies the standard commercial markups to generate the 
final tender labour price. Based on the existing Jeffway structure, this includes the Labour Markup of 
26.5% (NI & Holidays), Office Overhead of 14%, and Net Profit Margin of 9.9%. These percentages are 
stored centrally and can be updated at any time. 
●​ Step 2.6: Operative Pricing Sheet Generation: The agent generates the 'Labour Prices' sheet that will 
be issued to operatives on site. This lists every plot number, house type, tenure (OM/Affordable), 
specification (Gold/Silver/Shared Ownership/Rented), and the agreed price-work value for each of 
the 10 fix stage categories. This is the sheet the lads use to know what they get paid per plot per fix. 
●​ Step 2.7: Margin Pot Calculation: The agent calculates the difference between the labour figure 
included in the tender (what the client pays) and the price-work figure issued to operatives (what the 
lads get paid), highlighting the retained margin per plot and per fix stage. This gives the commercial 
team instant visibility of the labour margin pot. 
●​ Step 2.8: Human Override Checkpoint: The agent highlights the suggested labour pricing in the 
spreadsheet, pausing the workflow to allow the estimator to review and adjust for known complexity 
factors (e.g., three-storey properties, difficult access, non-standard layouts, timber frame vs. masonry 
differences). The estimator provides final approval before the workflow proceeds. 
Module 3: Supplier Quoting & Comparison 
●​ Trigger: The estimator approves the material quantities and labour pricing from Modules 1 and 2. 
●​ Step 3.1: Quote Request Generation: The agent extracts the consolidated material list from the

product descriptions, manufacturer product codes (e.g., Deta SD1209CHW for a 2G Switched Socket),

●​ Step 3.2: Supplier Selection & Dispatch: The agent references the stored supplier list to identify the 
designated wholesalers for this project. While three suppliers is the standard approach, the agent can 
dispatch to up to six (from the pool of Templegate, Eddis & Walsall, KEW, Medlock, Moss, and Rexel) 
if requested. The agent automatically emails the quote requests to all selected wholesalers, 
requesting pricing to be returned in the provided format within a specified timeframe. 
●​ Step 3.3: Quote Ingestion & Format Normalisation: As suppliers return their completed sheets (via 
email), the agent ingests each response. Because each supplier uses different column formats (e.g.,

from the main package comparison. It can also route specialist items to sub-contractors for pricing

●​ Step 3.10: Final Population & Handover: Upon final supplier selection by the commercial team, the

Module 5: Timesheet Processing & Labour Tracking 
●​ Trigger: An operative emails their weekly handwritten PDF timesheet to the Contracts Manager, who 
checks and approves it before forwarding to the finance inbox. 
●​ Step 5.1: OCR Data Extraction: The agent uses a dedicated OCR engine (such as Google Document AI 
or Azure Document Intelligence) to read the handwritten PDF timesheet. It extracts the operative's 
name, employee reference (e.g., JWE262), site code (e.g., 2168 Ph6), plot numbers, descriptions of 
work, and the hours or price-work amount claimed for each line item. The two-step OCR-then-GPT 
approach gives significantly better accuracy than vision alone on handwritten or inconsistently 
formatted timesheets. 
●​ Step 5.2: Terminology Normalisation: The agent passes the OCR text to GPT-4o to normalise 
inconsistent wording. For example, 'PL2' is mapped to 'Plot 2', 'finals' is mapped to 'Finals & Heating', 
and abbreviations are resolved against the project's known 10 fix types (Ducting, 1st Fix, 2nd Fix, 
Finals & Heating, Fit Appliances, Weather Compensator, 20mm Condensate Pipe, Test, EV Charger, 
Garages). 
●​ Step 5.3: Site Code Validation: The agent verifies that the site code on the timesheet matches a live 
project in the system (cross-referencing against the 'Current Works' tab in the Wages Summary). If 
the site code is unrecognised, the line item is flagged for manual review. 
●​ Step 5.4: Plot & Fix Availability Check: The agent checks the master 'LABOUR' tab for the relevant 
project to confirm that the specific plot and fix type have not already been claimed by another

operative. If the cell is already populated with another operative's initials, the agent flags this as a 
potential duplicate claim and generates an alert. 
●​ Step 5.5: Amount Verification: The agent compares the amount the operative is claiming against the 
agreed price-work value for that fix on that plot type (from the Labour Prices sheet generated in 
Module 2). If the operative is claiming more than the allowed amount, the agent flags the 
discrepancy. If they are claiming less (e.g., partial completion), the agent logs it as a partial claim. 
●​ Step 5.6: Day Work Handling: For operatives working on hourly rates or day rates (common on larger

there is an accompanying daywork sheet reference. If no daywork reference exists for hourly work, 
the agent flags the item to be reverted back to the operative. Validated day work is logged against 
the communal labour budget rather than the plot-based labour tab. 
●​ Step 5.7: Discrepancy Report: For any line items that fail validation (duplicate claims, overcharges, 
unrecognised site codes, amount mismatches, missing daywork references), the agent compiles a 
discrepancy report and emails it to the finance team. Each flagged item includes the operative's 
name, employee reference, the claim details, and the specific reason for rejection. 
●​ Step 5.8: Labour Tab Population: For all validated claims, the agent inputs the operative's initials and 
the claimed amount into the correct cell on the master 'LABOUR' tab for the relevant project 
application spreadsheet. This automatically feeds into the main application sheet (as the labour tab is 
linked to the application). 
●​ Step 5.9: Wages Summary Update: The agent simultaneously updates the individual operative's tab 
on the master JWE Wages Summary spreadsheet (e.g., the 'Alex Hanley' tab, the 'Andy Bond' tab). It 
enters the site codes, plot numbers, and amounts under the correct week-ending date columns, 
maintaining the existing 81-row x 22-column structure per operative. 
●​ Step 5.10: Summary Tab Aggregation: The agent updates the master 'Summary' tab on the Wages 
Summary, ensuring the Total Gross, Standard Hours, Price Work, Holiday/ROP, Bank Holiday pay, 
Phone, Van, and Mileage columns are all correctly aggregated for each operative. It also updates the 
'Timesheet Checklist' tab to confirm receipt and processing. 
●​ Step 5.11: EQUE2 Preparation: The agent compiles all validated timesheet data into the exact format 
required for direct entry into the EQUE2 payroll system, which feeds through to Sage Payroll. This 
includes the gross pay breakdown, salary sacrifice pension deductions, and the net payable amount 
per operative. 
●​ Step 5.12: Performance Analytics: The agent continuously logs completion data against historical 
averages. It flags management if specific operatives are consistently taking longer on comparable 
plots or house types (e.g., if Operative A is claiming 2nd Fix on a Crocus in 2 days but the average is 
1.5 days). It also flags if certain plot types are proving more expensive in labour than the tender 
allowed. This data feeds back into Module 2 for future tender pricing accuracy. 
●​ Step 5.13: Underpayment & Overpayment Alerts: If the cumulative labour spend on a project is 
tracking significantly above or below the tendered labour allowance, the agent sends a proactive 
alert to the commercial team, enabling early intervention rather than discovering the issue at project 
completion.

●​ Step 6.2: Order Reference Parsing & Normalisation: The agent reads the order reference on each 
line item (e.g., '2145 11 LLANYRNEWYDD', '2226/STONEWOOD RETURN', 'TW NAILSEA', '2168 PV') 
and maps it to the correct project site code, plot number, and fix type. Where operatives or site 
foremen have used inconsistent formatting, the agent applies normalisation logic to resolve the 
reference. 
●​ Step 6.3: Project Spend Allocation: The agent allocates each line item to the correct project in the 
master Project Spend Tracker. It logs the material cost against the specific project, building a 
cumulative spend picture. 
●​ Step 6.4: Price Verification: The agent cross-references the charged unit price on each line item 
against the agreed tender price established with the chosen supplier in Module 3. If the supplier has 
charged a higher price than agreed (e.g., due to an unapproved price increase), the agent flags the 
item with the exact financial impact calculated. 
●​ Step 6.5: Discount Verification: The agent checks that the correct discount percentage has been 
applied to each line item. Suppliers sometimes fail to apply the agreed discount, resulting in 
overcharges. For example, if KEW has agreed a 70% discount on cable glands but only applied 65%, 
the agent flags the difference and calculates the financial impact. 
●​ Step 6.6: Quantity Monitoring: The agent compares the cumulative quantity of each item purchased 
for the project against the total quantity allowed in the original takeoff. If the site has ordered 150 
double sockets but the takeoff only allowed for 120, the agent flags the overrun and calculates the 
cost of the excess. 
●​ Step 6.7: Unrecognised Purchase Detection: The agent identifies any line items with order references 
that do not match a known project or plot. These are flagged as potential unauthorised purchases 
(e.g., operatives buying materials for personal use on the company account). The agent generates a 
separate alert for these items. 
●​ Step 6.8: Returns & Credits Processing: The agent identifies negative quantities (returns) in the 
supplier statements and reconciles them to ensure the project spend is accurately reduced. It tracks 
handling charges on returns and ensures they are accounted for. 
●​ Step 6.9: Monthly Exception Report: The agent generates a comprehensive monthly exception 
report for the finance team, organised by project. The report includes: items charged above the 
agreed rate, missing or incorrect discounts (with the exact financial impact), quantity overruns by 
item, unrecognised or suspicious purchases, and a project-level summary comparing total material 
spend to date against the tendered material allowance. 
●​ Step 6.10: Supplier Credit Note Tracking: When the finance team reverts back to the supplier for a 
credit based on the exception report, the agent tracks the outstanding credit notes and reconciles 
them against subsequent statements to ensure the credits are actually applied. 
●​ Step 6.11: Profitability Dashboard Update: The agent updates the project profitability view, 
combining the material spend data from this module with the labour spend data from Module 5,

Module 7: Application Template Generation & Monthly Invoicing 
●​ Trigger: A project is confirmed as won and marked as 'Awarded' in the system. 
●​ Step 7.1: Application Template Creation: The agent takes the final tender revision spreadsheet and 
automatically generates the Main Application spreadsheet (e.g., 'JWES Main Contract Application'). It 
creates the SUMMARY tab with the Account Summary, the JWES Application tab with the formal 
interim application letter format, and the Main Application tab populated with every plot number, 
house type, and the agreed value per fix stage (1st Fix, 2nd Fix, Finals, Garages, Car Charging). 
●​ Step 7.2: Fix Stage Percentage Setup: The agent sets up the tracking columns for each fix stage, 
configuring the spreadsheet to track percentage completion (0 to 1) and monetary value claimed to 
date. The percentage split between fix stages is configurable and stored centrally. 
●​ Step 7.3: Communal Area Setup: For flat developments, the agent sets up the communal area 
section below the flat plots, with percentage-based claiming for each communal work package 
(Containment, Lighting, Small Power, Distribution, AOV, Door Entry, etc.). 
●​ Step 7.4: Variations Tab Setup: The agent creates the 'Variations to Contract' tab, pre-formatted with 
columns for Item number, Plot, Description, Order Number/Ref, Works Value, % Complete, Claimed 
to Date, Paid, Outstanding, and Notes. This is ready to receive variation entries as they arise during 
the project. 
●​ Step 7.5: Labour Tab Setup: The agent creates the 'LABOUR' tab, pre-populated with every plot 
number and fix stage from the tender, ready to receive operative timesheet data from Module 5. 
●​ Step 7.6: Monthly Template Duplication: At the start of each application period, the agent 
automatically creates the new month's application (e.g., moving from Application 19 to Application 
20). It copies the 'Gross Claimed to Date' figures into the 'Previous Claim' column, resetting the 
current month's claim to zero, ready for the contracts manager to update. 
●​ Step 7.7: Contracts Manager Input Workflow: The agent sends the current month's template to the 
relevant contracts manager(s), requesting them to update the percentage complete for each plot and 
fix stage. When the contracts manager returns the updated sheet, the agent ingests the highlighted 
plots and updated percentages. 
●​ Step 7.8: Claim Calculation: The agent calculates the current month's claim value based on the newly 
updated completion percentages. It produces the Gross Valuation, less Previous Valuations, to arrive 
at the Valuation This Month figure. It also calculates the Net Balance Due after deducting amounts 
already paid. 
●​ Step 7.9: Variation Logging: As new variations arise during the project (e.g., customer extras like 
additional USB sockets at £54 per plot, drawing error corrections backed by daywork sheets like 'DW 
5165', return visits, or compound works), the agent logs each one into the 'Variations to Contract' tab 
with the description, agreed value, order reference, and claim status. 
●​ Step 7.10: Client-Specific Timing: The agent manages different application submission dates per 
client. For clients requiring actuals (e.g., 28th of the month), it only includes confirmed completions. 
For clients requiring projections (e.g., 19th with month-end valuation), it includes confirmed 
completions plus projected works based on the contracts manager's forecast. It also tracks the 'Final 
Date for Payment' for each application.
